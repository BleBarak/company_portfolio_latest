How we turned an old hoverboard into a robot base

  • Robo City Tech Team
  • 8 Nov 2026
  • 7 min read

Every robot that moves needs a base: motors, a battery and something to control the motors. Dedicated robot motor controllers work well, but they are expensive, especially here in Zambia once shipping is added. We had a few old hoverboards lying around, and a hoverboard already has everything a robot base needs: two strong hub motors, a 36 V battery pack and a mainboard that drives both wheels.

So we asked: can we teach the hoverboard to take orders from our own electronics instead of its foot sensors? The answer is yes.

Step 1: find out which board you have

Hoverboards come with different mainboards. Ours had a single board for both motors with an STM32F103RCT6 chip, the type the open-source community has done the most work on. Some hoverboards have two separate boards, one per wheel, and those need a different approach.

Step 2: unlock and reflash the chip

We connected a cheap ST-Link V2 programmer to the board's programming header and used OpenOCD to talk to the chip. Factory boards are read-protected, so the chip has to be unlocked first. This erases the original firmware for good, so the board will never work as a hoverboard again. After that it can be reflashed as often as you like.

For the new firmware we used the excellent open-source hoverboard-firmware-hack-FOC project by EFeru. All the hard work is theirs. We changed the settings so that the board takes speed commands over a serial cable and sends back its battery voltage, temperature and wheel speeds.

Two things surprised us. On programmer power the board beeps non-stop: it is the low-battery alarm, because it sees no battery. And after flashing it plays a tune and switches itself off, for the same reason. Connect the battery and it comes to life.

Step 3: wire it to an ESP32

The board has two 4-wire cables that used to go to the foot-sensor boards. We use the left one as a serial port. The wire colours are not standard, so we checked ground with a multimeter and tried both directions for the data wires. One wire carries 15 V. It must never touch the controller, or it destroys it, so our adapter cable leaves it out completely.

On the other end is a Seeed XIAO ESP32-S3. Its small screen shows its network address, and it sends speed commands to the board many times a second while it reads back the telemetry.

Step 4: drive it

The ESP32 serves a simple driving page that you open on your phone over Wi-Fi. It also pairs with a PS4 controller over Bluetooth. A Raspberry Pi can drive the board over the same serial link too.

  • Wheels off the ground for every first test
  • Start with a low current limit, then raise it once everything behaves
  • Treat the 36 V lithium battery with respect, and never charge it unattended

What it is for

This base is now the foundation for our robotics work. It is cheap, powerful and repairable, and it puts real robots within reach of schools and makers in Zambia. Next we are mounting a depth camera on it so the base can map rooms and find its own way.
